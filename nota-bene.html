--- title: Nota Bene permalink: /nota-bene/ ---
  1. Judging AI models runs into the joint hypothesis problem (Fama, 1970). The output depends on both the input and the model's ability, so a bad answer does not reveal which of the two failed.
  2. The most common complaint about AI models is that they lack "taste." Part of the reason is that people mean very different things by taste. A model cannot meet a standard that has not been described to it.
  3. For jobs that use AI, the best signal is something a candidate has built with AI and can show: a hackathon project, a side project, a tool made at work. A walk-through of that project, including where it broke and how the output was checked, reveals more than a course certificate.
  4. In an ideal world, writing would be judged only on its quality. But reading takes time and effort, so readers look for cheap screens, and AI detectors have become one: a fast way to decide whether a piece deserves a careful read. Like any cheap screen, a detector makes mistakes in both directions. Careful human writing gets flagged as AI (false positives), and edited AI writing passes as human (false negatives). A screen like this can help decide what to read first, but it is a poor basis for punishing the writer. See also Mahoney (2026) on writing as a costly signal that AI makes cheap.
  5. Most people building frontier AI think alike, partly by design. In a race where every single employee counts, labs hire people who already believe in the mission, and disagreement slows things down. The cost appears when the consensus is wrong: few people on the inside are in a position to say so.
  6. Blockchain, arguably the hype before AI, spent years rediscovering why finance looks the way it does: why custodians, clearinghouses, and know-your-customer rules exist. AI will likely go through the same exercise. Many of the problems AI agents now run into (whom to trust, how to verify work, who bears the loss when something goes wrong) are old problems in finance and economics, with a large body of work behind them.
  7. The financing side of AI gets far too little attention. Most of the conversation is about models and what they can do. But data centers are being paid for with bonds, private credit, and leases that sit off balance sheet, and someone ends up holding that risk. If the payoff arrives later than expected, who holds the risk will matter more than which model is best. See also Van Nieuwerburgh (2026).
