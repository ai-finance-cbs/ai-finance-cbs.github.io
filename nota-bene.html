--- title: Nota Bene permalink: /nota-bene/ ---
  1. For employers: what kind of people should I hire for jobs that use AI? Look for people who have built something with AI and can show it: a hackathon project, a side project, or a tool they made at work. Ask them to walk you through it, including where it broke and how they checked the output. That tells you more than a course certificate does.
  2. When we judge AI models, we run into a joint hypothesis problem (Fama, 1970). What AI gives you depends on both your input and the model's ability. So when the output is bad, you cannot tell whether the model is weak or your input was. Ask a model for a funny joke about life as an MBA and you get a stale one. Tell the same model what makes a joke good, or give it a collection of essays by working comedians on how they build jokes, and it gets much funnier.
  3. The biggest complaint about AI models is that they lack "taste." But that is probably because, by definition, people have different definitions of taste. A model cannot hit a target that you have not described to it.
  4. It is not surprising at all that models started by solving math problems. A math problem says exactly what a right answer is, and anyone can check it. For the same reason, when the work is important, start with tasks where you can easily check the output. If you cannot check it, you cannot trust it (for now).
  5. What are good resources to stay on top of AI? Techmeme has the day's tech news on one page. On Twitter/X, where most new work shows up first, follow Gavin Baker, Paul Kedrosky, Dylan Patel, Marc Rubinstein, Byrne Hobart, Joe Weisenthal, Simon Taylor, and Anton Korinek. Good podcasts are Exchanges (Goldman Sachs), Invest Like the Best, Unhedged (FT), BG2Pod, and AI in Financial Services. Good newsletters are Net Interest, The Diff, Paul Kedrosky, Epoch AI, SemiAnalysis, and Fintech Brainfood.
