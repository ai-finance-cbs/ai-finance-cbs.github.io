--- title: AI Takes permalink: /ai-takes/ ---
  1. Judging AI models runs into a joint hypothesis problem (Fama, 1970). What you get out depends on what you put in and on what the model can do, so a bad answer doesn't tell you which of the two failed. I show this in class with a simple demo. Ask a model for a funny joke about MBA life and you get something stale. Tell the same model what makes a joke good, and the joke gets a lot better.
  2. The most common complaint about AI models is that they lack "taste." I think that's partly because people mean very different things by taste. If you can't describe the standard, it's hard to blame the model for missing it.
  3. If I were hiring for a job that uses AI, I'd look for people who have built something with AI and can show it: a hackathon project, a side project, a tool they made at work. Ask them to walk you through it, including where it broke and how they checked the output. That tells you far more than a course certificate.
  4. It has become fashionable to run someone's writing through an AI detector and, if it gets flagged, to cancel the writer. I think this is completely absurd. The detectors are unreliable, and careful human writing gets flagged all the time.
  5. Much of the AI world, and the AI safety community in particular, holds strongly correlated views. People read the same things, have the same friends, and share the same implicit assumptions, so they also make the same mistakes. Some of this is by design: when you are in a race and every single employee counts, you hire for conviction, and dissent becomes expensive. But as any portfolio manager knows, diversification has value of its own.
  6. Blockchain, arguably the hype before AI, spent a lot of its time rediscovering how society already works, especially finance: why we have custodians, clearinghouses, and know-your-customer rules. I expect the same with AI. Contract theory, public choice, law, management, and finance already have answers to many questions that the AI world is now working out from first principles (and sometimes that's fine!). The AI side should be less dismissive of the "old world," and academics should be less dismissive of AI progress.
  7. We don't pay nearly enough attention to how AI is financed. Most of the discussion is about models and capabilities, but the build-out is now a capital markets story: data centers funded with debt, private credit, and off-balance-sheet vehicles, with banks increasingly exposed. Every general purpose technology has had a correction, and who holds the risk when it comes matters as much as how good the models are. See also Van Nieuwerburgh (2026).
